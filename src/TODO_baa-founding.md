Leicester Chronicle - Saturday 30 March 1844

New Archaeological Association. — We have great pleasure in announcing the formation, under the most powerful and promising auspices, of a new society, to be called the "British Archaeological Association for the encouragement and prosecution of researches into the arts and monuments of the early and middle ages, particularly in England." It is to be under the direction of a central committee resident in London.

The want of such an active institution of this kind has long been a reproach to the country, and caused the irreparable loss of many a precious 'relic of antiquity. Its professed objects are "to investigate, preserve, and illustrate all ancient monuments of the history, manners, customs, and arts of our forefathers, and, in furtherance of the principles with which the Society of Antiquaries of London was established, to render available the researches of a numerous class of lovers of antiquity who are unconnected with that institution."

Exertions are being made to issue the first No of *The British Archaeological Quarterly Journal*, which will be a record of all the proceedings, towards the end of March. No fixed plan of pecuniary contribution has as yet been arranged. On the contrary, it is at present voluntary; but we understand it is proposed to hold, at appointed times, a Historical Congress, something after the manner of the British Association, on which occasion, we presume, there will be some call for the "sinews ot war." It is proposed that the assemblage should be made at some place remarkable for its historical monuments and other objects of antiquity; and we believe that Canterbury or Winchester will be fixed upon for the present year.— Abridged from the *Gentleman's Magazine*.

It is rumoured that the British Archaeological Association, whose views and objects we lately brought before the attention of our readers, contemplated holding their first Congress Canterbury. West Kent Guardian - Saturday 13 April 1844

Literature.

NOTICES OF NEW WORKS.

*The Archaeological Journal. Published under the direction of the Central Committee of the British Archaeological Association for the Encouragement and Prosecution of Researches into the Arts and Monuments of the Early and Middle Ages.* —No. 1.

This publication is the first-fruit of an Association, of the formation of which we have already spoken in terms of especial satisfaction, and to the operation of which fostering and extending that taste for the study of antiquity, which is now becoming prevalent, we look forward with confidence. The opening paper of the publication is very properly an introductory one, somewhat in the form of a public manifesto on behalf of the Committee, explanatory of the objects of the Association and of the proposed means of attaining those objects. In this respect, however, this introductory paper contains little information additional to that conveyed in the statement published on the formation of the Society a few weeks since and then copied into our columns. The proposed course of action is, indeed, more fully and minutely developed, and appears to us to be eminently calculated to secure the very desirable objects iv contemplation, so far as they can be secured by any co-operation of private individuals. Doubtless in some points improvements may be suggested for an Institution of so novel a character must necessarily be at first imperfect, and time and experience will best intimate to an observant and active Committee such alterations as may conduce to the attainment of the purposes designed.

In perusing the paper under consideration ooe cannot fail to perceive on the part of the writer a prominent nervous anxiety to avoid the appearance of any thing like rivalry between the Society of Antiquaries and the new Association. This feeling seems to us unnecessary. Kindred as are the objects contemplated by the two societies, they move, nevertheless, in courses distinct from and perfectly independent of each other. The new combination meditates objects which, "from peculiarities in its construction" could not be efficiently pursued the older association; and a similarity in principle and feeling, whilst there is no identity of pursuit, should engender a spirit of co-operation and a desire for mutual promotion of purpose. It is most satisfactory, indeed, to find that this spirit and desire prevail, —that the Archaeological Association has received the "concurrence of the officers of the Society of Antiquaries, and is favoured by the sanction and patronage of its most distinguished members;" but it must not hence be inferred that the Association is an offshoot of the venerable Society at Somerset House, with operations merely and "wholly subsidiary" to those of its eldest sister. It is only by preserving the elasticity of independence and that vigour which is compatible only with perfect freedom of action, and vigilantly maintaining that unrestrained course on which it has been launched, that the Association will be enabled to attain those high practical results to which its efforts are directed. Thus, also, will it be in a position — when the proper moment shall arrive, and the Government shall be disposed to do its duty in this respect, — to become, with the sanction and efficient aid of the Executive authorities, a recognized national Institution.

A very proper practice has been adopted by the Committee for conducting the *Journal*, in requiring that the signatures of the writers shall be affixed to their respective articles. We thus find that the responsibilities of this introductory paper devolve upon the influential and honoured name of Mr. Albert Way, who is associated with Mr. C. Roach Smith in the Honorary Secretaryship of the Society.

The number contains several admirable papers, which however, the rigid restriction of newspaper limits precludes us from analysing; including one on Numismatics, by Mr. C. Roach Smith; Painted Glass, by Mr. C. Winston; Anglo Saxon Architecture, by Mr. T. Wright; Bell Turrets, by the Rev. J. L. Petit; the Mediaeval Antiquities of Anglesey, by the Rev. H. L. Jones; Cross-legged Effigies, Mr. W. S. Walford; the Horn-shaped Ladies' Head-Dress the reign of Edward 1, by Mr. T, Wright; Catalogue of the Emblems of Saints, by the Rev. C. Hart; &c; with a Report of the Proceedings of the Committee; Notices of Archaeological Publications, and other matters of interest.

Several of the articles are illustrated by etchings, and numerous wood-cuts; and the publication altogether is one of high interest, conveying much information aud many valuable practical suggestions, and it forms a very satisfactory commencement of a series of volumes indicating the labours and registering the results of an useful and important Association.

The PRESIDENT then said,— We have now to proceed to the more important business of making the regulations for our guidance in the future, and there is one point of considerable importance to which I will now direct your attention, as it is one on which may arise misconception or misconstruction. We have been ourselves put to great inconvenience, and the public generally have been put to great inconvenience— to use a vulgar and old saying—by there being two Simon Pures in the field. ("Hear," and laughter.) It is inconvenient to persons wishing to join us,—it is inconvenient to parties wishing to join somebody else,—it is inconvenient to all, and seeing the way in which we have been supported by the public they are, I think, entitled to consideration at our hands, and I therefore think we ought to change our name. I have thought of this before, and just before going abroad had a conversation with Mr. Way respecting it, to see if we could not arrange it before another meeting. I recommended to our rivals,—not that I mean to call Lord Albert Conyngham my rival, for I believe that his intentions are of the best kind, although I am afraid he has allowed himself to be deceived,—I wrote to him, recommending that both, by common consent, should change our names, and that as there were two words to the present title — Archaeological Association — we should take one word and they the other; that one should be called the Antiquarian Association, and the other the Archaeological Society. I will read to you Lord Albert's reply, which I think most honourable to him individually. I am sorry to say I cannot read you my letter to him. I unfortunately did not preserve a copy of it. The Marquis then read Lord Albert's letter, the summary of which is, "that he could not well make the Marquis's proposition to members of an association who had just elected him their president, as by so doing, they would admit they had assumed a title without any claim to it. They were willing to listen to any proposal for re-uniting the society, but such proposal must come from the other side. He however was willing to make any personal sacrifice to secure such object." To this the Marquis rejoined, "that he was afraid any attempt to unite the bodies at present would be more likely to prevent than produce so desirable an object; that he did not wish the other party to make any concession, for if it was a concession on one part, it must have been on both. The reason he had suggested that the first step should be taken by them was because they held their meeting first, and would thus have the first opportunity: besides, Lord Albert was president of his section, while he (Lord Northampton) was only the local president elect of the other. He did not intend that either party should abandon their claim to be the association, but simply for mutual convenience each to give up part of their common name." I did not succeed, but my feelings still remain the same. We do not call upon you to make any concession to the other party, but to look to the public convenience; that public who have so generously supported us on the present occasion, and who have a right to say, "Why put us to this inconvenience? Why make matters personal that ought not to be personal? Why talk of the Way party and the Wright party?" We are now strong. We can stand upon our own ground. (Cheers.) We can say to Lord Albert, "You are the minority; the name is of no consequence to us, you may have it." (Hear, hear.) We are 700. (Loud cheers.) Under these circumstances I deny that we are making any concession, and if we were, we can afford to make it. We do not say we are not in the right, for I believe we are. We were right in not consenting to the violent measures taken at the time, but our opponents always avoid the real question at issue. Lord Albert Conyngham resigned the presidency, and this put us into a difficulty. There are times when it is necessary for public bodies to use violent means, but they should always be careful of being more violent than is absolutely necessary. Now, in this case, admitting, for the sake of argument, there was a grievance to be redressed, all that could be necessary was that the general committee should be called upon to call a general meeting of the members. However, they held a meeting, at which about 150 out of 1,700 or 1,800 members attended. No notice was given that they, the minority, intended to turn out the majority of the committee; but an intimation rather to the contrary. What right then had they to turn them out? None. But we had a right to say we would not abide by the decision of such a meeting; and mind another thing,—the meeting took place before Easter—three weeks or a month before any one was in London. A meeting so called had no power to re-elect Lord Albert Conyngham. We will not now go into the question of the Album; admitting (for the sake of argument) there had been mistakes, they do not justify such a proceeding. However, the change of name does not admit the correctness of their proceeding; the only parties concerned are ourselves and the public, and I think the latter have a right to expect thus much at our hands. (Cheers.) I must now refer to a statement by Mr. Pettigrew published in The Times to-day. He says, "I cannot but deeply regret to see a nobleman for whom I entertain the highest respect standing forth as the leader of the secessionists, and in his speech, as reported in your paper of this day, he is represented to describe himself as 'one of the earliest members that joined the association, and afterwards filled the situation of president of the architectural section.' Now, Sir, this must surely be an error of your reporter, for the Marquis of Northampton never attended a meeting of the association, never proposed either a member or a correspondent, never subscribed to the funds,—nay, even declined to be president of the central committee upon its formation, on the ground of his position as president of the Royal Society. The only architectural section ever held was at Canterbury, and Professor Willis was the president." In regard to my being one of the earliest members of the association, I believe I was, though I never paid any money previous to the split, owing to the irregular management of the association. Soon after the split I accepted the office of president, and I did so to mark my opinion as president of the Royal Society. I did it because I thought it necessary to mark an irregularity most dangerous as a precedent. (Loud cheers.) The reporter is wrong in saying I "filled" the situation of president of the architectural section. I "accepted" the office, and my name appeared in print as such; but afterwards, upon accepting the office of president of the society, I vacated that of president of the section. I believe I was one of the earliest members, I do not mean to say one of the first 12, but that I joined the association months after it was formed. I gave a donation. (Here some gentleman said that the donation of the Marquis was the second on the list—"Hear, hear.") It is true I never attended any meeting, because there never has been more than one meeting. I fully intended to have been present at it, but going abroad, I was prevented. In fact, my health would at that time have hardly allowed me to attend. To return, however, to our regulations. The committee have come, after great consideration, unanimously to the determination to change our name and adopt a fresh one. It is not one of the names I recommended to Lord Albert; but still it will show I was sincere in my offer, and will not in any way detract from our position. We are to be called the Archaeological Institute of Great Britain. The word "institute" is, I think, a better name than "society," and it is borne by one of the leading bodies of Europe—I mean the Institute of Paris. The word implies that we mean to teach, and that we are not merely a company met together for the sake of society. (Hear, hear.) I think it will be a very dignified name. There will be no difficulty in regard to our journal—the name will remain the same. The next number of the Archaeological Journal will be No. 7; the last was No. 6. You are now called upon to confirm the decision of the committee; you, of course, have a perfect right to negative the decision of that committee. This, I trust, you will not do; but place that trust in them which I think they have deserved at your hands. (Cheers.) So far we have had a prosperous voyage, and are nearly in port, where I hope we shall arrive safely. With these observations I trust I have made my farewell speech to the controversy, and that we shall have no more of it. If absolutely necessary to defend ourselves, of course we must not shrink from it; but, as we shall abstain from any attacks upon them, they will abstain from further attacks upon us. (Cheers.)

London Evening Standard - Wednesday 17 September 1845

BRITISH ARCHAEOLOGICAL ASSOCIATION. WINCHESTER, Tuesday.

A general meeting was held yesterday, at St. John's House, Winchester, for the purpose of submitting the rules and regulations under which the association will in future be conducted, for confirmation; also for the election of the governing central committee; the production of a general statement of the accounts, and the selection of the place of annual meeting for the ensuing year.

The Marquis of Northampton having taken the chair,

The Rev. 8. R. Maitland read a statement, from which it appeared that the sums expended, on account of the association, amounted to 187*l.* 17*s.* 3*d.*, and that the balance in the banker's hands to the 8th of September, amounted to 369*l.* 6*s.* 6*d.* The amount actually received at Winchester was about 160*l.* or 170*l.* The number of subscribing members was upwards of 700.

Mr. Way then read the report, which stated that the association had excited great interest in every part of the kingdom, and many gentlemen of eminence in the literary and antiquarian circles were about to join it. He announced that amongst the donations received for the library, the Chevalier Bunsen had presented his recently published Dissertation on the Basilicas of Christian Rome, and their connection with the theory and history of church architecture.

The Noble Chairman observed that now was the time to enter upon the more important business of the day. Great inconvenience had been felt, not only by the members, but by the public generally, in consequence of there being two *Simon Pures*. This produced great inconvenience to persons in the country, who wished to join this association, and it was of importance that the difficulty should be got over. He had in consequence written to Lord Albert Conyngham, the president of the other society; and in reply his lordship, alter acknowledging the receipt of the letter, said that, on reflection, he could not propose to the members that they should change the name of their association, because, by doing so, it might be afterwards said that they had assumed a name to which they were not entitled. His lordship, in conclusion said he was desirous that the societies should be united and in that event he would resign the presidency. In answer to this letter the noble marquis stated that he had written to assure the noble lord that all the members of the Archaeological Association would appreciate his anxiety for a union, and entertained but one opinion of his zeal for archaeological science. He proposed no concession to the noble lord; but that for mutual convenience they should each give up part of their common name. This was not acceded to; still, though he was not successful in his proposition, Ids feelings on the subject remained the same, and the committee before whom lie brought it forward agreed with him in opinion. He had not called on the other association to make any private concession; but he had called upon them to make a concession to the convenience of the public, who had a right to expect it. The feelings of the committee and his own were so strong on this ground that— notwithstanding Lord Albert Conyngham's determination, and having been joined by 700 members— under such circumstances, if it were deemed to be a concession, which he denied, the association could afford to make it. The committee had thought it very advisable that the name of the association should be changed, and that it should be called the Archaeological Institute of Great Britain.

The rules and regulations for the government of the institute (22 in number) were then read, by which it is provided that it shall consist of life members, contributing a donation of not less than 10*l.*; of annual members contributing not less than 1*l.* each year, and of corresponding members, who, taking an interest in the objects of the institute, and being disposed to give furtherance to them, without any pecuniary contribution, may desire to attach themselves to the institute. The corresponding members are not entitled to vote nor to have any other privileges. Each annual meeting is to be held in one of the cities or principal towns in the kingdom, at which the elections, the appointment of the place of meeting for the ensuing year, &c., shall take place, notice of such meeting to be given by one of the honorary secretaries. The election of secretaries and corresponding members to be made by the committee on the proposal of one of the members thereof, either on his own personal knowledge, or on the recommendations of two subscribing members of the institute.

Ray, Amber M. "British-ness and the National Archaeological Societies: 1840-1890."


By the 1840s many had become disenchanted with the Society of Antiquaries in
London (Soc. Ant.) created in 1707 (Díaz-Andreu, 2007: 54), and found their selection
of members to be questionable. It was decided that another organization would provide
fruitful results and could work alongside the objectives of the Soc. Ant. Apart from the
politics of such a selective group as the Soc. Ant., the new organization could hold
greater membership, with less strict guidelines on admission. In 1844 the British
Archaeological Association (BAA) was formed and held its first summer meeting at
Canterbury. At this inaugural event the president of the new society, Lord Albert
Denison Conyngham, spoke about the motives for its establishment:
“… the Archaeological Association had been formed, purposing to embrace a more numerous
class of persons, and to enter upon a wider field of active research, than that to which the
exertions of the Society of Antiquaries have hitherto been directed” (AJ, 1845: 269).
By 1845, however, the organization had split into two distinct groups. One
organization, the portion of membership supporting Thomas Wright, kept the name of
the BAA, while the other, followers of Albert Way, called itself the Royal
Archaeological Institute (RAI) (Wetherall, 1994). The Way faction (the RAI) would
continue to publish its proceedings and papers under the Archaeological Journal (AJ),
from which the argument had initially emerged, whereas, the Wright faction (the BAA)
would publish their proceedings under a new title, The Journal of the British
Archaeological Association (JBAA). In 1845 the two organizations held separate
summer meetings at Winchester. For each year thereafter the two national
organizations held annual summer meetings at selected locations throughout the United
Kingdom. Their influence over the foundation of local county archaeological,
architectural and otherwise antiquarian societies is well known (Levine, 1986). Less
well-defined, however, is the influence the BAA and RAI had over the refinement of 
12
archaeology as a discipline, how they aided in the progression of various identities, and
the establishment of a uniquely British nationalism.
