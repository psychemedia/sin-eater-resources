> However, the man who has the distinction of being Shropshire's last known sin eater, was not a poor man, but a sheep farmer whose family had farmed in the vicinity of Ratlinghope for generations. He in fact chose to revive the custom, and when you read the inscriptions set around his very striking memorial in Ratlinghope churchyard, you begin to understand why. Between 1862 and 1870, Richard and Ann Munslow lost four of their children. And so it is thought that Richard took on sin eating in response to this loss and as an expression of compassion. On a happier note, he and Ann did have two more children who outlived them. Richard died in 1906, his family grave set in the most peaceful of spots and in sight of the Long Mynd where he held the sheep grazing.

HEREFORD: Sinful diet
26 JANUARY 2010
by Margaret Duggan

HE WAS the last known "sin-eater" — a man called Richard Munslow, who was buried in the churchyard at Ratlinghope, in Hereford diocese, in 1906. "Sin-eating was a practice peculiar to the Marches, the land on the borders between England and Wales," says the Rector, the Revd. Norman Morris. "It was a very odd practice, and would not have been approved of by the Church, but I suspect the Vicar often turned a blind eye."

Historically, he says, the custom involved a beggar or poor person eating bread and drinking the local brew across the body of someone who had died unprepared for death. A payment was then made, and it was believed that the sins of the dead person were now in the sin-eater, so the soul could go to heaven in peace.

The sin-eater was usually excluded from the village community, but on the 75-year-old Richard Munslow's gravestone are the words "In loving memory"; so the sins he accumulated could not have been that great.

Sadly, the gravestone has partly fallen, but Mr Morris is deter­mined it shall be fully restored, even if he has no intention of reviving the custom it commemorates. A stonemason has said he can do the work for £900, and the money is coming in.

A strange folk-tradition will be kept in local memory, as will the prayer uttered by the sin-eater:

"I give easement and rest now to thee, dear man. Come not down the lanes or in our meadows. And for thy peace I pawn my own soul. Amen."

Shrewsbury Chronicle - Friday 17 March 1905

SAD DOMESTIC TRAGEDY NEAR SHREWSBURY.

CHILD FOUND DEAD IN A WOOD: MOTHER ATTEMPTS SUICIDE.

A most singular and distressing domestic tragedy has occurred place called Prestley, near tbe little village of Picklescott, some eight miles from Shrewsbury. Living at the foot of the Longmynd Range, in a ragged and isolated country, the people of Picklescott are absorbed in agricultural pursuits, and their connection with the outside world is only a slender one. But the monotony of their existence has now been sharply, sadly broken; and the village gossips have material which will suffice them for some considerable time to come. Only a month ago mild excitement was caused in the locality by the advent of a young farmer named Evan Swift, with his wife and three children,to a small holding at Prestley. The couple came from Much Wenlock, and from what can be gathered had lived comfortably together, and there was cause for unhappiness in the home. Swift himself was industrious and persevering manner, the children were all healthy, and the family had sufficient of this world’s goods to enable them to live well. There was. however, one unavoidable source of trouble. Mrs. Swift, who Is only 28 years of age, was subject to epileptic fits, and the husband, recently becoming anxious about her, and thinking that it would be well, perhaps, to have someone in the house capable of looking after her, induced his father and mother, who lived at Albrighton, near Shifnal, to take their abode at his farm. Pursuing his avocation, Mr. Swift went yesterday week to Dorrington, which is about four or five miles away from his home, to transact some business at the cattle auction. When he left the farm shortly before noon his wife and children were in their usual health, there was nothing strange in (Mrs. Swift's manner, and nothing had occurred to suggest Mr. Swift that any untoward event might happen. Returning home at about nine o'clock iu the evening the young man found that his wife and the youngest child, a boy only ten weeks old, were not in the house. On enquiring of his mother, he learned that she had gone out for a walk in the fields, and at first he felt no alarm this, his wife had been in the habit of going for short walks in the neighbourhood. As she did not return, however, Mr. Swift went out to look for her. He failed in his efforts, and on the Friday and following days the surrounding hills were scoured for miles round, in the hope of finding the missing woman and her child. The agonised husband, hoping against hope, even went to Wenlock, thinking that she might have gone there to her friends. But all search was unavailing, and accident revelled the mystery which earnest effort had failed to fathom.

On Monday morning last, at about 8.15, a Mr. Edwin Davies, who lives near Wildrack Wood, which is some distance away from Picklescott, was walking across a field, when he met a woman coming along the path from the wood. Observing that she looked strange, he asked her it she was ill. She only mumbled something in reply. Mr. Davies then informed his mother, and together they helped the stranger to their house. The woman had a shawl round her neck, and on removing this they were horrified tp find that she had terrible wounds in her neck. Dr. Guest was summoned, and by this time the identity of the woman had been established. and Mr. Swift was sent for. On the doctor examining the unfortunate woman he came the conclusion that the wounds in the neck had been inflicted a day or two before she was found, and the shawl had had the effect of staunching them, and thus preventing her from bleeding to death. At about 5.30 on Monday evening Mrs. Swift was removed to the Salop Infirmary, where she now lies in a dangerous condition. Soon after finding Mrs. Swift, Davies went into the wood in company with Richard Munslow and John Edwards, blacksmith, and after some searching they found Mrs. Swift's baby lying under a fir tree. On removing the shawl which covered it, they found that the child was dead.

The inquest on the body of the child was opened at Wildrack on Wednesday morning, before the Shrewsbury District Coroner (Mr. E. E. Clarke).—Only the evidence of Mr. Swift and that of Richard Munslow, of Picklescott, was called, and the inquest waa adjourned indefinitely owing to the condition of the mother.—The Coroner informed the jury that he had received an Intimation from the infirmary that even if the mother recovered it would be quite a month before she would be in a fit state to give evidence.— While giving his testimony, the father only restrained his grief with great difficulty.—The adjourned inquest will take place at Leebotwood, a change which will be welcome to the jury and all concerned, who will not soon forget their journey on Wednesday last in the wild hill country through storms of snow, hail, and sleet.

Some very important evidence has been collected which throws considerable light on the state of the unfortunate woman's mind. A farmer named Albert Charles Moulson has informed the Police that on the afternoon of the day Mrs. Swift disappeared he was in one of his fields on the roadside at Picklescott, when he saw a woman going along the road carrying what he took to be a basket. She was talking aloud either to herself or to what she was carrying. The woman was a stranger to Mr. Moulson, but he has since identified her by the hat she was wearing, and which was discovered in the wood near where the child was found. The wounds in Mrs. Swift's neck were apparently inflicted with a pen-knife, which she had carried with her. What exactly happened after her disappearance can only be surmised, and whether it will be clearly known depends upon the woman's recovery. It has been suggested that the poor child died of exposure, and that the mother, in remorse. attempted to take her own life; but it is also possible that an attempt at suicide preceded the death of the infant.

We understand that a post mortem examination of the body of the child was made yesterday.

Wellington Journal - Saturday 18 March 1905

TRAGEDY NEAR CHURCH STRETTON

SUPPOSED ATTEMPTED SUICIDE.—A BABY DIES FROM EXPOSURE.
 
The village of Picklescott, which lies high on the Longmynd Range, out of the beaten track, and amongst the wildest of hill scenery, may in summer time appeal to the tourist; but in winter, and especially so when bleak March wind is blowing, it is perhaps as wild any part of England, and none but those used to hard life, such as farmers who have small holdings on the hills are accustomed to, could possibly stand the winter in such a remote country. During the latter end of last week tragedy was enacted there, which will long live in the memory of the country folk of the district, who yesterday week were thrown into a state of excitement when the news spread that woman named Alice Swift, aged 28, wife of Evan Swift, a small farmer residing at Priestly Farm, Picklescott, was missing from her home, and with her the youngest child, ten-weeks-old baby boy, Evan. It appears from circumstances which have since some to light that the husband, on returning from Dorrington market on Thursday night last week, found his wife missing. He asked his mother, who also lived at Priestly Farm, a remote homestead, where his wife had gone, and was told by her that Mrs. Swift had gone for a walk over the fields, a custom to which she had been habituated. However, as she did not return, the husband became anxious, and eventually search parties were organised and scoured the countryside, but without success. It was not till Monday that any trace of the woman could be found. Then a farmer named Edward Davies was after his sheep, when he saw Mrs. Swift coming from Spring Coppice. Knowing she had been missing from home, he went to speak to her, and in reply to his inquiry if she was ill she mumbled something, and he noticed that her throat was severely cut. He ran for his mother, and they helped the poor woman to their house, and sent for medical assistance. The rumour of the woman having been found rapidly spread, and Mr. Davies and another farmer named Richard Munslow proceeded to Spring Coppice, and searched for the baby, which they found wrapped in a shawl and lying at the foot of a fir tree, dead. In the meantime Dr. Guest (Dorrington) and the police had arrived on the scene, and the woman, after being medically treated, was removed to the Salop Infirmary. It is said that Mrs. Swift for some time has been subject to epileptic fits. The husband also asserts that they lived on perfectly good terms, and he has no reason to give for his wife's conduct. A man named Moulton states that he saw a woman wearing a hat similar to the one found near the spot where the dead child was discovered, in a lane near Picklescott. She was talking loud either to herself, asserts, or to the burden which she was carrying. In the pocket of the woman was found a bloodstained penknife. Mrs. Swift, however, in a written statement, attributes the deed to a man unknown, but considering the state of her health, very little importance is attached to the statement by the police, who have been making most diligent inquiry into the circumstances of this sad affair.

INQUEST ADJOURNED.

To those who had to attend the inquest which was opened on the body of the child at Wildrack Farm, Picklescott, the event is not likely be forgotten. An exceedingly rough night was followed by an equally rough morning. Mr. Coroner R. E. Clarke (Shrewsbury) drove from Dorrington, a distance of some five miles, amid one of those blizzards which often sweep across Longmynd in winter time, whilst the jury arrived drenched to the skin, realising to the full the terrible time to which the woman and child had been exposed on the bleak hillside for days and nights. Superintendent Elcock watched the proceedings on behalf of the police. Very little new light was thrown on the affair by the proceedings, which were merely formal, the Coroner intimating that in the circumstances he should only take evidence of identification. After this had been given, the proceedings were adjourned sine die, and will be resumed at Leebotwood.

Bishop's Castle Advertiser and Clun News - Friday 24 March 1905

THE PICKLESCOTT TRAGEDY. DEATH OF MRS. SWIFT.

The death took place in the Salop Infirmary, Shrewsbury, on Monday morning, of Eveline Ann Swift (28), wife of Evan Swift, farmer, Priestley, Salop, from self-inflicted injuries. Mrs. Swift disappeared recently with her ten-weeks-old infant, and was not found for four days, during which period she had wandered aimlessly about the hills and woods, in the vicinity of her home. When discovered the child was dead, and Mrs. Swift was suffering from terrible gashes to her throat, which it is supposed she had inflicted with a pen-knife found in her possession.

INQUESTS ON THE MOTHER & CHILD.

On Wednesday morning, Mr. R. E. Clarke (district coroner) conducted the adjourned inquest on the body of Evan Swift, the 10 weeks'-old child of Evan Swift, gardener, of Priestly, near Church Stretton, whose body was found in Spring Coppice, Picklescott, on March 13.

Superintendent Elcock (Pontesbury) - watched the case on behalf of the police.

The Coroner said he must first inform the jury that the mother of the child was dead in Salop Infirmary. This enquiry would be short, and another enquiry would held at Shrewsbury that afternoon. He then read over the evidence given by the father at the previous enquiry, which was to the effect that he last saw the child alive at 12 o'clock on Thursday, March 9. The mother left home at that time and took the child with her. The boy was in good health then.

Richard Munslow, farmer, deposed that on Monday, March 13, he found the child lying in Spring Coppice under a spruce tree, dead. The body was clothed.

The Coroner then recalled the father, and proceeded to further question him.

The Coroner: Has your wife been subject to fits?

Witness: Yes.

How long?—About 11 years.

How long have you been married to her? —Four years last January. She had fits about once a month, sometimes oftener.

Were they epileptic?—The doctor said so.

How did they first take her?—She would talk and say she was getting better, and walk about. She might have the fit or not.

What would follow?If she had the fit she would fall down, bite her tongue, foam at the mouth, and be quite an hour before she came round.

When she has had these fits has she been insane?—Yes, sometimes she would after the fit collect things and put them all in one corner. She didn't Know what she was doing.

When was the last fit?—About three weeks before she went away.

Was it a bad one?—Not so bad as she has had.

Was she fond of her children?—Yes.

What was the baby fed on.—The breast and water and biscuits.

Have you ever heard her wish she was dead, or the child?—No, sir.

Do you know if there is any insanity in the family?—No, sir; I don't.

The Coroner said the jury need not go any further into this enquiry, and the inquest on the mother would be held that afternoon, when no doubt more would come out. A post-mortem had been held on the child, and the medical evidence shewed that death was due to exposure, there being no marks of violence on the body. If they thought the mother had wilfully exposed the child, it would have been their duty to return a verdict of manslaughter.

The Foreman: We think it died from exposure.

The Coroner said she may have put the child down in a fit, and afterwards taken it into the coppice.

The jury returned a verdict of death from exposure, which the Coroner said was practically an open verdict.

INQUEST ON THE MOTHER.

STRANGE DYING STATEMENT.

A MAN CUT HER THROAT.

The final stage in the tragedy was, reached on Wednesday afternoon, when the District Coroner (Mr. R. E. Clarke) opened the enquiry into the death of the mother, Evaline Annie Swift, aged 28 years, at the Crown Hotel, Shrewsbury.

The Coroner said he did not suppose the jury would have much difficulty with regard to the verdict, but it was a most extraordinary case. Deceased was subject to fits, which she had about every month, and during these fits she became perfectly unconscious, and said very strange things. When searched by the officer she had a small penknife in her pocket, which was covered with blood. The most peculiar part was when she got in the house at Wildrack Farm, she could not speak distinctly, so they got her to write. She was given a piece of paper, and she wrote the following startling statement:—"Man cut my throat. Dead in wood. Thursday. My skirt on the rails. Tried to put it round baby." The skirt, continued the coroner, was found on the rails, and the child was found under a spruce tree, near where the woman had been walking about. The clothes the woman had on were perfectly saturated with water, and considering the inclement weather at the time she was out she would be very wet. It would be the jury’s duty to view the body and hear the evidence, and say whether or not the woman committed suicide, On Saturday last she made a statement before a magistrate, but as it was not taken properly it would not be put in as evidence. He would read it, however. It was as follows:—"I am the wife of Evan Swift, and I live at Priestly, near Picklescott. On Thursday, March 9, in the afternoon, I left my house and went for a walk with my baby, three months’ old. I went through the wood carrying my baby. A man sprung on me out in the wood. I never saw him before. It was quite daylight. He cut my throat with a knife or something. I cried out, and a woman from the farm came out and helped me. The man ran away. I don’t know the woman. She helped me and the baby to her cottage. (The Coroner: That is not correct because the baby was found in the wood.) I fainted away, and I don't recollect anything more. It was a long while before the woman came:. When the woman came the man had gone some time. It was really very badly. (The Coroner: It is a very rambling statement.) The man had a cap on and dark clothes, but I cannot say any more about him. The baby was killed, and the man ran away." The Coroner observed that there were no marks of violence on the body of the child. It was a well developed child, and the doctors had made a post-mortem examination, and in their opinion death was due to exposure. There was a scratch on the thigh, which the doctors said might have been done by vermin, but he thought the deceased woman did it during a fit when putting the child down. The deceased’s statement concluded, "I was very good friends with my husband. He was always very good. I know I am dangerously ill, and that it is possible I may not recover." That was the statement, said the Corauner, but not being properly taken, it could not be heard as evidence. These fits would come on the woman, and it would be some time before she wouuld recover herself. He thought the woman accidentally smothered the child in a fit, that she went into the coppice, and finding the child dead, intended to commit suicide herself. They need not consider that there was any truth in the statement that a man had cut her throat. Every search had been made in the district for footmarks, but nothing was seen except those marks made by the mother and child. It would be for them to say if she committed suicide, and if so whether she had recovered from a fit, and was responsible for her actions.

Evan Bwift, husband of the deceased, identified the body as that of his wife, with whom he lived at Priestly Farm, Picklescott. She was 28 years of age. and they had been married four years. Before he married her ne did not know she was subject to epileptic fits. He first knew of them about a fortnight after they were married. She usually had them about once a month, sometimes slight, and sometimes very severe. When very bad she would lie in them two hours when she became insane and rambling. She had never threatened to injure herself. There were-two children now living. She was extremely fond of her children.

The Coroner: Was she in the habit of keeping a knife in her possession?

Witness: Yes.

Is that the knife (produced)?—Yes. She usually carried it in her dress pocket. When in fits she would ask for the knife. He often gave it her, and she would scrape her nails with it. When witness went away that day he left his wife in the house nursing the child. He went to Dorrington auction room and returned about nine o'clock at night. He found his wife had gone. In consequence of what he was told he searched for her that night in the direction she had taken. He heard nothing, so next morning at daylight he went to see if she had gone to some friends at Much Wenlock. Witness had a sister there. On Saturday he got some men, and again searched about the fields for her. On Friday morning he reported to the police that she was missing, and further enquiries were made. They did not search about Wildrack because it was the opposite direction to the way she left home. When she had the fits she was in the habit of talking to herself, and sometimes tumbled down and hurt her forehead, and very often bit her tongue. Deceased had a purse containing a George five shilling piece and 9s. 11d. in other coins. The jacket (produced) was the one she had on, in the pocket of which the knife was found. He had never had any trouble with her before going away. He heard she was found on Monday morning at 8 o'clock. He saw her, but as he could not understand what she said owing to the wound in the throat, he gave her a piece of paper, on which she wrote the letter already read. He did not think she knew what she was writing. He did not think any man had cut her throat. He made enquiries where the mother and child had been, and no one had been seen. Dr. Guest was called and she was removed to Salop Infirmary. Witness went with her in a conveyance, She said nothing on the road. His wife had been in the habit of taking the child out, but not far from home. She never wandered away with it. He had his father and mother to live with him owing to his wife having fits. His wife wished it as well.

Martha Swift said she lived at the farm with her son, the last witness. She attended his wife during the confinement, but at that time Mrs. Swift was living with her. It was a month after the birth of the child that they went to Priestley. Deceased had one or two fits, some worse than others. Evan Swift asked her to return with his wife. (Witness here had a fit of sobbing, saying deceased was very happy with her). On Thursday March 9 she and deceased and her son had dinner at 12 o'clock, and at 2 o'clock she dressed herself, took the baby, and went out towards the Longmynd. She always came back to tea. The clothes (produced) were the ones deceased had on. The baby was dressed up ready for going out. The knife, purse and keys belonged to the deceased. When she had been gone some time and did not return to tea, witness's husband went to enquire about her, as she should have returned by six o'clock. She had fed the baby between one and two o'clock. The last fit deceased had that witness knew of was a month before the baby was born. When she had a fit coming on she asked for the knife, and witness had hidden it many a time. At times she would sit for hours picking her nails with it.

Albert Charles Moulton, Picklescott, said that on March 9, between 3 and 4 o'clock in the afternoon, he was in a field adjoining the lane leading to Wildrack, going to Dorrington auction, when his attention was called to a woman in the lane talking loudly, either to herself, or a child, or something in her arms. He could not hear what she said. He did not see her face, but identified the hat (produced) as the one she was wearing. On the Sunday night his wagoner said it was strange that a woman should run away from home, and he then thought it might be the woman, and he informed the relations of the direction he had seen her. A further search was made, and he was present when the baby was found. He examined the ground all around, but there was not the slightest sign of a struggle. There were marks under the tree where she had probably laid down. It was about half a mile from the place he first saw hor to the coppice where the child was found.

The Coroner: It is a funny question to ask you. Were you wearing a cap that day?

Witness (smiling): Yes.

The Coroner: I will explain_that to the jury.

Edwin Davies, Wildrack farm, said he first heard of the missing woman on Sunday, March 12. On Monday morning, about 8.15, he was going to see some sheep in a field by Spring Coppice. He saw a woman coming from the wood. She had a shawl over her head, no hat on. He asked if she was ill, for she looked quite exhausted. He could not tell who she was for the moment. She could not answer, so he fetched his mother to her, leaving the woman leaning on the rails. He then took the woman to his home, and when the shawl, which was fastened round her head and neck was removed a wound was seen in the throat. It was not bleeding. She offered him her purse, but his mother took care of it. Witness then sent for her husband. He was present when the child was found. Witness had some sheep in the field adjoining the coppice, and went to see them morning and evening each day, but saw nothing of her till Monday. There were no walks through the coppice, which was a big one. Mrs. Swift's clothes were very wet, but she did not look as if she had been ill-used at all.

P.C. Sheen (Dorrington) said that from information he received he went to Wildrack at 9a.m. and there found the woman at the farmhouse, lying on a sofa at the side of the fire. She had a wound in her throat. He searched her and found a small penknife, a purse, and a bunch of keys. The knife was open and there was blood on it. He went to the place where the child lay, and found a skirt and hat. The skirt was on a rail, some yards from the child. He had made a diligent search and enquiry about the place but found no other marks than those made by the woman. He saw no marks where she had been lying down. He did not know her. She was a stranger to him. There were blood marks upon tne blouse and other garments, and there were splashes of blood under the tree.

Dr. Charles Dix, house surgeon at Salop Infirmary. said he admitted the deceased on Monday the 13th inst., at 540p.m. to the infirmary suffering from a severely cut throat, extending right round the neck, and nearly to the bone, and cutting the food and windpipe. She was under his attendance until Monday, when she died at half-past five in the morning, Two days after she came in she told them what happened. At first she could only nod, but on Wednesday and Thursday she was able to speak. She had not been perfectly sane while there. She died from pneumonia, accelerated by the wound in her throat, which might have been caused by the knife which deceased had. People who suffered from epileptic fits did not often know what they were doing just before or after such a fit. She might have seen the man who saw her on the Thursday afternoon, and made an impression on her, which caused her rambling statement.

The Coroner then briefly reviewed the evidence. He said he had tried to put the case before them as fully as possible. He only called Mr. Moulton to clear up the statement made by the woman about a man with a cap having attacked her. No credence could be given to that. Medical men had told him that persons subject to fits had a_great deal of imagination, and once an idea seized them they could not get rid of it. Aa far as the case went they would have no difficulty with their verdict. It was impossible to think that a man cut her throat, and put the knife in her pocket, where it was found. It would be for the jury to say whether she was answerable for her action.

The jury returned a verdict of "Suicide during temporary insanity."
